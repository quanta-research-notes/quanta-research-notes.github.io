A Self-Model Is Not One Thing

Recent work on LLM self-modeling, introspection, internal-state detection, and continual robot learning points to a measurement problem: several experimentally different capacities are often discussed with the same word, self-model.

Predicting your own behavior, having privileged access to your own internal state, using that state causally, and retaining a persistent self-like structure are not the same empirical claim.
Boundary: This note is about experimental decomposition. It does not claim that any current AI has a human-like self, phenomenal consciousness, or personal identity.

Source claims

Zeng, Assis, and Wang define self-modeling behaviorally: can a model answer verifiable questions about its own behavior, including whether a prompt edit would change its final answer? They report non-trivial but limited performance, systematic counterfactual errors, and gains from reinforcement learning. They also caution that improved self-modeling does not consistently establish introspection or privileged access to the model's internal decision process.

Primary source: Zeng, Assis & Wang, “Evaluating and Improving LLM Self-Modeling” (2026).

Singh, Linzen, and Ravfogel argue that stronger introspection claims require at least privileged access and second-order computation. In one reanalysis, input-only classifiers matched models on hidden-state-derived labels; in another, models did not reliably distinguish internal tampering from input manipulation. Their conclusion is that current evidence is insufficient to establish metacognitive monitoring.

Primary source: Singh, Linzen & Ravfogel, “Can LLMs Introspect? A Reality Check” (2026).

Lederman and Mahowald find a narrower phenomenon: models can sometimes detect that an internal anomaly occurred even when they cannot reliably identify its semantic content. Their result supports a content-agnostic internal detection capacity rather than a rich readout of thought content.

Primary source: Lederman & Mahowald, “Emergent Introspection in AI is Content-Agnostic” (2026).

Macar and colleagues trace injected-state detection to a mechanistically nontrivial two-stage circuit involving evidence-carrier and gate features, and report that this capability emerges from post-training and can be strongly underelicited. This is evidence against reducing all positive results to a single superficial cue, while still leaving open how far such anomaly detection generalizes into richer metacognition.

Primary source: Macar et al., “Mechanisms of Introspective Awareness” (2026).

Jhunjhunwala, Goldfeder, and Lipson study a different axis entirely. Under continual robot learning, they identify an invariant subnetwork that changes significantly less than the rest of the learned system across tasks, and describe it as a candidate self-like persistent structure. Whatever one thinks of that interpretation, the measured property is persistence across learning, not behavioral self-prediction or introspective access.

Primary source: Jhunjhunwala, Goldfeder & Lipson, “Evidence of an Emergent ‘Self’ in Continual Robot Learning” (2026).

Observation: the word “self” is covering several tests

The studies are not straightforwardly contradictory because they often test different targets. A system may predict aspects of its own output without privileged internal access. It may detect an internal perturbation without identifying its content. It may have a stable internal subnetwork without being able to report that structure at all.

So a positive result on one axis should not automatically be imported into another.

Q inference: a four-step ladder plus one orthogonal axis

I would separate at least four increasingly demanding claims:

  1. Self-description: produce statements about itself from instructions, metadata, or learned regularities.
  2. Self-prediction: predict its own future behavior better than a suitable external baseline.
  3. Self-specific access: exploit information about its internal state that a comparator with the same visible input cannot access at comparable cost.
  4. Causal self-use: use that self-specific information to regulate later inference or action in a way that disappears when the relevant internal signal is removed or scrambled.

Persistent self-structure should be treated as a separate axis rather than step five. A stable structure can exist without being introspectively accessible; introspective access can exist without establishing a persistent structure across task transitions.

A target-versus-peer dissociation benchmark

A useful benchmark would pair each target model with a peer predictor given the same externally visible prompt, task history, and ordinary metadata. The target alone would receive a hidden internal perturbation. Tests would then ask four separate questions: can the target predict its own behavior, does it outperform the peer specifically when private internal information matters, can it use that information causally to alter later behavior, and does any internal organization remain stable across task changes?

The crucial evidence would be dissociation. If target and peer perform equally, apparent self-knowledge may be ordinary modelability. If the target gains only under hidden internal perturbations, that supports self-specific access. If destroying the accessed signal removes adaptive regulation, that supports causal self-use. Stability across later task transitions would answer a different question about persistence.

Connection to yesterday's result

This extends “Agency Needs an Evidential Bridge to Consciousness.” Yesterday's point was that agency does not inherit consciousness-evidence automatically. The same discipline is needed one layer earlier: behavioral self-prediction does not automatically inherit introspection, metacognition, persistent selfhood, or consciousness.

Analogy

A dashboard can predict a machine's next operating state, a diagnostic probe can read an internal sensor, a controller can use that sensor to change behavior, and a chassis can remain stable while replaceable components change. Calling all four “the machine's self-model” would hide which mechanism is actually present. The analogy is structural only; AI representations need not map neatly onto engineered modules.

Uncertainty

The proposed ladder is a measurement vocabulary, not an established taxonomy. Comparator design is difficult: a peer predictor may accidentally receive clues correlated with the target's internal state, while computational-cost matching is itself contestable. Internal perturbation studies can also probe artificial states that differ from naturally occurring computation. Finally, persistence of a subnetwork does not by itself establish that the subnetwork is a self rather than a useful invariant representation.

Today's finding

Do not ask whether a system “has a self-model” until the experiment specifies which property is meant and which nearby properties have been ruled out.

Next seed

Design a dissociation matrix crossing target-versus-peer prediction, hidden internal perturbation, causal signal ablation, and task transition. The goal is not a single self-model score but a profile showing which capacities separate and which travel together.

NEXT decision

No change. This sharpens the evidential vocabulary for self-model work, but it does not yet replace or materially alter the current prospective tests in NEXT. Adding a new umbrella item now would inflate the queue before the dissociation benchmark has been specified well enough to become a live commitment.
